Dexterity from Supervised Play

Learning dexterity from minutes of goal-agnostic human play. No task demonstrations, no sim2real.

The five stages of DSP

Stages 1 to 4 make up the full system. Stage 5 is an optional speed-up.

1Fifteen minutes of human play

An operator wearing a Manus glove drives the Sharpa hand and plays with a tagged cube for about 15 minutes. There is no task and no reward. The operator keeps the cube moving without dropping it.

We train a diffusion-transformer play policy on these recordings with behavior cloning. Given the recent observations, it samples a chunk of future joint commands. Choices about keeping contact and switching between grasps are already in the demonstrations, so nobody has to write safe play down as rules.

data
~15 min goal-free teleoperation
policy
diffusion transformer, behavior cloning
output
chunks of 22 joint-position targets

2Autonomous play on the robot

Next we run the play policy on the robot with nobody at the glove. It keeps turning and regrasping the cube, and every step is logged as a transition. Because no person is needed, the amount of data is limited by robot time, and collection could run on several robots at once.

Our scaling study uses between 0.54 and 5.11 hours of this autonomous play.

3A world model trained on play data

From the play data we train an action-conditioned world model. Given the last C observations and a candidate action chunk, it predicts the next K observations of the cube. We train it with Diffusion Forcing, which gives every timestep its own noise level. The model conditions on a clean history and denoises the future causally, so it can roll a prediction forward step by step under whatever action chunk the planner wants to test.

last C observations + action chunk
output
next K cube poses
training
Diffusion Forcing

4Planning with play proposals

To reach a goal, we sample 1,024 action chunks from the play policy and roll each one through the world model. A chunk's score is the closest its predicted trajectory gets to the goal orientation. The robot executes the first part of the best chunk, observes the result and plans again.

The goal appears only in this score. The play policy and the world model never see it, which is why the same two models serve any goal without retraining. One replan is shown step by step below.

5Optional policy distillation

Every replan rolls all 1,024 proposals through the world model. To avoid that cost at run time, we also run the planner inside the world model, starting from recorded states with sampled goals, and keep the action prefix it chooses. Training on these labels with behavior cloning gives a goal-conditioned policy without any robot time or physics engine.

The distilled policy is several times faster than a full MPC replan. In simulation it keeps over 90% of the planner's success at 0.8 rad and gives up more at tighter thresholds.

How DSP compares

Success rate on the real hand

A random walk in joint space never reaches the goal, and a random walk in cube orientation at the play policy's speed rarely does. The play policy alone gets within 0.6 rad of the goal in 38.3% of trials. Because the orientation random walk turns the cube just as fast, that gap points to the structure of the learned contacts and regrasps.

Planning on top of play raises success at 0.6 rad to 90.0%. At every threshold, the hardware result stays within 4.1 points of DSP in simulation.

Skills the planner selects from play

We never demonstrated these skills as tasks. They are motions from the play repertoire that the planner picks because their predicted outcome moves the cube toward the goal.

Simulation: imitation and RL baselines

Simulation lets us run baselines that are impractical on hardware, over 128 seeded trials with start and goal more than 90° apart. At 0.6 rad, DSP succeeds in 91.4% of trials. Hindsight BC, trained on the same 701K transitions with relabeled goals, reaches 53.9%. The play data holds useful motion, but relabeling alone does not turn it into goal-directed control.

PPO given the same 701K frames does not learn the task, and it needs 344M frames to reach 86.7%. With unlimited simulator frames, PPO reaches 96.9% at 0.6 rad and converges more finely than DSP.

Push-T: planar pushing from a 10k-frame teleoperation budget

Push-T asks a round pusher to move a T-shaped block onto a target pose. With the same data budget for every method, DSP succeeds in 98% of trials at 0.90 mask overlap, against 74% for hindsight BC, the strongest policy baseline. The gap widens at the stricter 0.95 threshold, and an RL policy trained on the same data does not learn the task.

Planning from pixels

We replace the tracked cube pose with camera images in simulation. The play policy sees RGB, and the world model scores proposals in a learned 64-dimensional latent space. At 0.6 rad, planning raises success from 53.1% for the RGB play policy alone to 78.1%. This experiment uses 64 trials and a 3,600-step horizon, so its numbers should not be compared directly with the state-based results.

Analysis

World-model error falls as autonomous play grows

We keep the model and the held-out set fixed and vary only the amount of autonomous play. Going from 0.54 to 5.11 hours lowers the 50-step rotation error by 18.8%.

Most of that gain arrives in the first two hours, and the error is still falling at five. The final model sits 26.4% below a baseline that assumes the cube does not move.

A diffusion play policy covers more transitions

We compared a deterministic transformer and a diffusion transformer as play policies. They imitate the operator about equally well on held-out commands (0.181 vs 0.192 command error), yet in closed loop the diffusion policy covers 84.2% of transitions where the deterministic one covers 55.4%. The deterministic policy reaches many of the same orientations through a narrower set of motions, which looks like mode averaging. Wider transition coverage gives the world model more varied actions to learn from and leaves the planner more ways to approach a goal.

Replanning takes 134 ms on one GPU

On one RTX 5090, a full replan takes 133.6 ms at the median. Most of it goes to sampling the proposals (60.5 ms) and rolling them out in the world model (44.2 ms). Cube tracking keeps up with the 30 fps camera.

The hand executes the current action prefix while the next plan is computed, so the closed loop runs at 15 to 18 Hz.

Questions about DSP

What counts as play here?

Goal-free interaction that keeps the object moving without dropping it, such as rolling the cube or passing it between fingers. The operator has no target orientation and no score to chase.

Our working hypothesis is that play can be modeled as a goal-agnostic policy that sees only the robot's recent observations, never a goal. Good play is hard to write down as rules, but under this view it can be learned with ordinary behavior cloning, and the habits that keep the hardware safe come along with the data.

Why not demonstrate the task directly?

Demonstrating play is much easier than demonstrating a dexterous solution. Turning a cube to an exact orientation through a glove is slow and error-prone, while any operator can play with it for 15 minutes.

We tried the direct route in simulation with a goal-conditioned BC policy trained on 15 minutes of goal-reaching teleoperation, the same human time as our play data. It reaches 34.4% at 0.6 rad, against 91.4% for DSP.

Why not reinforcement learning?

In our simulated benchmark, PPO with DSP's budget of 701K frames does not learn the task. It needs about 490 times more frames (344M) to reach 86.7% at 0.6 rad, close to DSP's 91.4%.

We did not run RL on hardware, where it would need millions of interaction frames and its early exploration could damage the hand. When frames are free, PPO does converge more precisely (96.9% at 0.6 rad with unlimited frames). DSP is meant for robots and tasks that have no such simulator.

Why use the play policy to propose actions?

Its motions are already feasible contact sequences, so the planner chooses among good motions instead of searching for them. Random motion is also a poor way to explore this hardware. Executed directly, a random walk in cube orientation at the play policy's angular speed reaches 11.4% at 0.6 rad, where the play policy reaches 38.3%.

Does DSP use simulation?

Not for the hardware results. The play policy and the world model for the real hand are trained only on human play and the robot's own autonomous play, and the planner runs on top of them.

We use Isaac Lab separately as a benchmark. It lets us run baselines like PPO and repeat 128 seeded trials per method.

What are the limitations?

We show two tasks: in-hand cube reorientation, from state and from pixels, and Push-T. The world model learns from what the play policy does, so its predictions are only trustworthy near the behavior that play produces. If play never produces a kind of motion, the planner has no way to propose it.

Planning costs compute at every step, and the distilled policy that removes the cost gives up precision at tight thresholds. When a simulator can supply unlimited frames, PPO still converges more finely. The pixel experiment runs in simulation with its own trial protocol. Play also still starts with a person, who has to provide those 15 minutes at the glove.

Could a large pretrained behavior model replace the human play?

Possibly. The play policy is a separate module, so a broader pretrained behavior model could supply the initial repertoire of interactions, with autonomous play grounding their consequences on a particular robot. We have not tried this yet, and we think it is a promising direction.

Will the code and data be released?

Yes. We plan to release the code, data and training details needed to reproduce the results, and we will link them at the top of this page.
